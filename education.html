Beginner Curriculum

Start Here: The Fundamentals

Five modules that take you from zero to confident investor, built from the best introductory material at each university.

1

What Are Financial Markets?

Sourced from Yale — Prof. Robert Shiller (ECON 252)

Understand why financial markets exist, who participates in them, and how stocks, bonds, and other securities actually work.

📚 5 lessons ⏱️ 2 hours 🌱 Beginner
  1. 1.1
    Why Financial Markets Exist Markets let people trade risk, raise capital, and invest savings. This lesson covers how markets channel money from savers to companies that need it — and why that matters for the economy.
  2. 1.2
    Stocks: Owning a Piece of a Company When you buy a share, you own a fraction of that company. Learn what stock prices represent, how companies issue shares through IPOs, and how supply and demand drive price changes.
  3. 1.3
    Bonds: Lending Money for Interest Bonds are loans you make to companies or governments. Understand face value, coupon rates, maturity dates, and why bonds are considered less risky than stocks.
  4. 1.4
    Market Participants & Exchanges Who's trading? Retail investors, institutional investors, market makers, and brokers. Learn how orders flow through exchanges like NYSE and NASDAQ to match buyers with sellers.
  5. 1.5
    Market Efficiency & Behavioral Finance Are markets rational? Shiller's Nobel Prize-winning research shows how human psychology — overconfidence, herd behavior, and fear — creates bubbles and crashes.

Key Takeaways

  • Markets exist to connect people who have capital with people who need it
  • Stocks represent ownership; bonds represent debt
  • Prices are set by supply and demand on exchanges
  • Human psychology plays a major role in market movements

2

The Time Value of Money

Sourced from MIT — Prof. Andrew Lo (15.401) & Wharton Corporate Finance

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow. Master the single most important concept in finance — and the math behind every investment decision.

📚 4 lessons ⏱️ 2 hours 🌱 Beginner
  1. 2.1
    Present Value & Future Value If someone offers you $1,000 today or $1,000 in a year, which do you take? Learn why money has a time dimension and how to calculate what future cash flows are worth right now.
  2. 2.2
    Compound Interest: The Eighth Wonder Einstein supposedly called it the most powerful force in the universe. Understand how compounding turns small, consistent investments into large sums over time — and how it works against you with debt.
  3. 2.3
    Discount Rates & Net Present Value How do companies decide which projects to invest in? NPV analysis compares the present value of all future cash flows against today's cost. If NPV is positive, the investment creates value.
  4. 2.4
    Applying TVM: Loans, Mortgages & Retirement Use time value of money to evaluate real decisions: How much do you need to save monthly to retire at 65? What does a 30-year mortgage really cost? These formulas have daily practical value.

Key Takeaways

  • Money today is worth more than the same amount in the future due to earning potential
  • Compound interest accelerates growth exponentially over time
  • NPV is the standard tool for evaluating any investment or project
  • These formulas apply directly to mortgages, retirement, and everyday decisions
Start this module on MIT OCW →
3

Reading Financial Statements

Sourced from NYU Stern — Prof. Aswath Damodaran (Foundations of Finance)

Every public company tells its financial story through three documents. Learn to read them — and spot the difference between a healthy business and a risky one.

📚 4 lessons ⏱️ 2 hours 🌱 Beginner
  1. 3.1
    The Income Statement Revenue, expenses, and profit — the income statement shows how much a company earned over a period. Learn to read revenue growth, operating margins, and net income to gauge profitability.
  2. 3.2
    The Balance Sheet Assets = Liabilities + Equity. The balance sheet is a snapshot of what a company owns and owes. Learn to assess financial health by examining debt levels, cash reserves, and shareholder equity.
  3. 3.3
    The Cash Flow Statement Profit isn't the same as cash. A company can be profitable on paper and still run out of money. Learn how operating, investing, and financing cash flows reveal the true financial picture.
  4. 3.4
    Key Ratios & Red Flags P/E ratio, debt-to-equity, return on equity, free cash flow yield. These ratios let you compare companies at a glance — and spot warning signs like unsustainable debt or declining margins.

Key Takeaways

  • The income statement shows profitability; the balance sheet shows financial position
  • Cash flow is more important than accounting profit for assessing real health
  • Financial ratios let you compare companies across industries and sizes
  • Always look at all three statements together — no single one tells the full story
Start this module on NYU Stern →
4

Risk, Return & Diversification

Sourced from Stanford GSB — Prof. Joshua Rauh & MIT Portfolio Theory

Higher returns come with higher risk — but smart diversification lets you reduce risk without sacrificing returns. The core insight behind modern portfolio theory.

📚 5 lessons ⏱️ 2.5 hours 🌱 Beginner
  1. 4.1
    The Risk-Return Tradeoff Historically, stocks return ~10% per year but can drop 30%+ in a bad year. Bonds return ~5% but rarely lose more than 10%. Learn why higher expected returns always come with more volatility.
  2. 4.2
    Measuring Risk: Volatility & Standard Deviation Risk isn't a gut feeling — it's measurable. Standard deviation quantifies how much an investment's returns swing around its average. Higher standard deviation means more uncertainty.
  3. 4.3
    Diversification: Don't Put All Eggs in One Basket Owning 30 different stocks is less risky than owning 1 — even if each individual stock is equally risky. Diversification works because stocks don't all move together at the same time.
  4. 4.4
    Correlation & the Efficient Frontier The magic of diversification depends on correlation. When assets aren't perfectly correlated, combining them creates portfolios with better risk-return profiles — the efficient frontier.
  5. 4.5
    Beta & Systematic Risk You can diversify away company-specific risk, but you can't diversify away market risk. Beta measures how sensitive a stock is to overall market movements — the risk that gets rewarded.

Key Takeaways

  • Higher expected returns always come with higher risk — there's no free lunch
  • Diversification reduces risk without reducing expected return
  • Correlation between assets determines how effective diversification is
  • Only systematic (market) risk is rewarded — diversifiable risk is not
Start this module on Stanford GSB →
5

Making Your First Investment

Sourced from Rice University — Investment Strategies & Stanford Mind Over Money

Put everything together. Set a goal, choose a strategy, and understand the practical mechanics of building a real portfolio.

📚 4 lessons ⏱️ 1.5 hours 🌱 Beginner
  1. 5.1
    Setting Investment Goals & Time Horizons Are you saving for retirement in 30 years or a house in 5? Your time horizon determines how much risk you can take. Learn to define clear, measurable goals before choosing any investment.
  2. 5.2
    Index Funds vs. Individual Stocks Warren Buffett recommends index funds for most people — and the data backs him up. Learn why broad market index funds outperform the majority of professional fund managers over the long run.
  3. 5.3
    Asset Allocation & Dollar-Cost Averaging Deciding how much to put in stocks vs. bonds vs. cash is the single biggest driver of returns. Dollar-cost averaging — investing a fixed amount regularly — removes the pressure of market timing.
  4. 5.4
    Common Mistakes & Behavioral Traps Panic selling in downturns, chasing hot stocks, overtrading — these behavioral mistakes destroy more wealth than bad stock picks. Learn to recognize and avoid the most common investor errors.

Key Takeaways

  • Define your goal and time horizon before choosing any investment
  • Index funds offer broad diversification at minimal cost
  • Asset allocation matters more than individual stock selection
  • The biggest enemy of investment returns is your own emotions
